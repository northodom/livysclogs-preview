Where Near North Side restaurants work

The Near North Side is one of Chicago's 77 community areas, listed as area 8 in the City's official boundaries (City of Chicago Data Portal). Our River North and Gold Coast pages cover those two neighborhoods. This one looks at the kinds of restaurant settings found across the wider area.

The Infatuation's Streeterville guide describes that neighborhood as the stretch "between Michigan Avenue and Navy Pier," which it says is "full of hotels, wandering tourists, and hospitals." Its picks include a restaurant inside a hotel, a restaurant inside the Museum of Contemporary Art, an upscale dining room on a hospital campus, a small spot "mostly meant for carry out," and a spot where guests sit at a communal table and watch the chef cook. One entry has a to-go area that the guide says caters to businesspeople "of both the suit-and-tie and scrubs variety" (The Infatuation: Streeterville and the Magnificent Mile).

The same publication's Gold Coast guide adds steakhouses, a seafood restaurant that it says shares a kitchen with a steakhouse, a café open for breakfast, brunch, and early dinner, and an Italian dining room with "a sea of white tablecloths" and "servers in tuxedo jackets" (The Infatuation: Gold Coast).

Put together, those guides show how different a shift can look from one Near North Side address to the next. A server might work a hotel breakfast, a museum lunch, or a white-tablecloth dinner. A line cook might work a quick carry-out counter, a kitchen that feeds two dining rooms, or a grill in full view of the guests. We don't know how any single restaurant runs, so treat the list below as a starting point.

The server's side of the door

For a server, the day is measured in trips: to the table, back to the station, through the door to pick up, and out again. Some rooms call for a uniform or a dress code, and some don't. A trip to the pass can take a server from carpet or wood onto kitchen tile and back again. Ask where your route goes before you choose a sole.

The line cook's side of the door

A line cook stays put and the work comes to them. Standing at one station for a whole service puts the weight on the same spot of floor for hours, and that floor picks up water, oil, and food as the tickets come in. Where the cooking happens in front of guests, the shoes are on show too.

What both of them need from a work clog

Read your employer's footwear rule first. Then compare against this list.

  • A closed heel that stays on. Every pick here is closed-back. Open-back styles leave the heel free, which some workplaces don't allow.
  • The grip your route calls for. A line cook's station needs a sole whose maker states a slip claim. If your employer names a standard, match it; ASTM describes F2913 as a lab test of the friction between a shoe and a floor (ASTM).
  • An upper that cleans up. Splashes happen on both sides of the door, so a wipe-clean finish saves time.
  • The maker's own fit advice. Each brand below explains its fit differently.

Livy's Clogs doesn't give medical advice. If your feet, knees, or back hurt after shifts, talk with a podiatrist.

Three closed-heel clogs for servers and line cooks

Birkenstock Profi Birki 2.0

This is the pick for the line. Birkenstock says the Profi Birki 2.0 was "designed with kitchen environments in mind," and its product page describes a lugged, certified slip-resistant outsole with self-cleaning, bone-shaped lugs, a waterproof and fluid-resistant polyurethane upper, a replaceable contoured footbed lined in microfiber, and regular or wide widths. It carries the European rating EN ISO 20347:2022 O4 SR FO (Birkenstock).

Keep in mind: the page names no US ASTM standard, so check whether your employer asks for one.

Sanita Professional clog

A traditional closed-back clog for a server whose route stays mostly in the dining room. On its Professional PU page, Sanita lists a padded instep, a microfiber-lined anatomically correct footbed, a lightweight rocker-bottom polyurethane outsole, and APMA approval (Sanita). The linked listing is the PU version, with a polyurethane-coated leather upper Sanita describes as easy-care, in a single standard width. Sanita's fit note calls for a pinky-sized heel gap, room for the toes, and a heel that lifts slightly out of the shoe with each step.

Keep in mind: this page makes no slip claim for the Sanita. Sanita offers ASTM-certified styles only in a separate collection (Sanita).

Dansko Professional

Another option for the floor. Dansko calls the Professional its "flagship style," and its product page lists a reinforced toe box with room to move, a contoured footbed, a padded instep collar, a heel counter for side-to-side stability, and an anti-fatigue PU rocker bottom, with a 2-inch heel and APMA acceptance (Dansko). Keep in mind: Dansko's product page makes no slip-resistance claim for the Professional, so it isn't the choice for the line or for any workplace with a slip rule.

Questions from both sides of the door

Should a server and a line cook buy the same clog? Not necessarily. They share the pass, but the line cook spends the whole shift on kitchen tile, while a server may spend most of it on the dining-room floor. Choose for where you stand longest, and for any footwear rule your employer sets.

What if my shift covers both the line and the floor? Then choose for the kitchen. The Birkenstock is the only pick here whose maker states a slip claim.
