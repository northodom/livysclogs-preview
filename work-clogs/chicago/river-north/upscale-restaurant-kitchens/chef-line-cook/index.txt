<h2 id="cooking-in-river-north">Cooking in River North</h2>
<p>Choose Chicago, the city&#39;s official tourism organization, calls River North an &quot;ultra-stylish, ultra-urban district of sleek art galleries and studios, all tucked away into former warehouse buildings.&quot; It also says the neighborhood is home to &quot;one of the city&#39;s hottest nightlife scenes,&quot; with bars and clubs &quot;that keep the party going till the early morning hours,&quot; and that River North &quot;shifts into high gear by night&quot; (<a href="https://www.choosechicago.com/neighborhoods/river-north/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Choose Chicago</a>).</p>
<p>Food is a big part of that picture. The same page says the dining scene &quot;is always buzzing,&quot; and it points to waterfront restaurants, classic steakhouses, and spots run by celebrity chefs. It also notes that theMART, one of the neighborhood&#39;s best-known landmarks, sits on the Chicago Riverwalk.</p>
<p>River North is one of the neighborhoods inside Chicago&#39;s Near North Side community area, according to the City Colleges of Chicago&#39;s community research guide (<a href="https://researchguides.ccc.edu/community_survey/Central" rel="noopener" target="_blank">CCC</a>).</p>
<p>What does that mean for your feet? We can&#39;t tell you how any single River North kitchen runs, and we won&#39;t guess at its hours. But if your restaurant serves the evening crowd that Choose Chicago describes, your shoes are on through the whole push, from setup to breakdown. A River North kitchen clog should earn its place on the busiest night of the week, not just on a slow shift.</p>
<h2 id="from-the-pass-the-chefs-side">From the pass: the chef&#39;s side</h2>
<p>A chef&#39;s shift covers the whole room. Between ordering, tasting, expediting at the pass, and checking on each station, a chef can walk the length of the kitchen again and again. Each lap can cross a different floor: damp tile by the dish area, a greasy patch near the fryer, the cold floor of the walk-in.</p>
<p>For a chef, the shoe question is partly about stamina and partly about the team. If the kitchen has a footwear rule, the chef may be the one who explains it to a new cook on day one.</p>
<h2 id="from-the-station-the-line-cooks-side">From the station: the line cook&#39;s side</h2>
<p>A line cook&#39;s shift is tighter. Grill, sauté, fry, or garde manger: the work happens in a few square feet, pivoting between the range, the board, and the reach-in. When tickets pile up, the pace goes up and there&#39;s little room to step around a spill.</p>
<p>A station floor collects splashes of water, oil, and sauce during service, and closing cleanup adds more wet tile. Cooks can work shoulder to shoulder, close to hot pans. A shoe that grips, stays put on the foot, and wipes clean at the end of the night matters as much on the line as it does at the pass.</p>
<h2 id="buying-criteria-for-a-kitchen-clog">Buying criteria for a kitchen clog</h2>
<p>Check your restaurant&#39;s footwear rule first, then compare listings against this list.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Grip on wet and greasy tile.</strong> Look for a maker that names a slip standard or test, then check it against what your employer asks for. You may see ASTM F2913, which ASTM describes as a lab test method that measures friction between a shoe and a floor to evaluate relative slip performance (<a href="https://www.astm.org/f2913-19.html" rel="noopener" target="_blank">ASTM</a>). A named test tells you how grip was measured. It doesn&#39;t mean a shoe can&#39;t slip.</li>
<li><strong>A closed heel and closed toe.</strong> A closed back wraps your heel, unlike open-back styles, and a closed upper keeps spills off the top of your foot. Need toe protection too? Then filter for a listing that states a safety-toe rating.</li>
<li><strong>An upper you can wipe down.</strong> Smooth, water-repellent uppers are simpler to wipe down after service than fabric that soaks up grease.</li>
<li><strong>Support for the long stand.</strong> Look for a contoured or anatomically shaped footbed, and read what the maker says it&#39;s designed for.</li>
<li><strong>The right fit and width.</strong> Clogs don&#39;t fit like sneakers. Read the maker&#39;s fit notes and check which widths it offers.</li>
</ul>
<p><em>This guide isn&#39;t medical advice. If your feet hurt after shifts, or you have a condition like plantar fasciitis, see a podiatrist.</em></p>

<h2 id="questions-from-the-kitchen">Questions from the kitchen</h2>
<p><strong>Why one list for both a chef and a line cook?</strong>
Because the floor doesn&#39;t care about job titles. The fryer spill a line cook stands next to is the same one a chef crosses on the way to the pass.</p>
<p><strong>Is &quot;certified slip-resistant&quot; the same everywhere?</strong>
No. Makers name different standards. Birkenstock&#39;s page for the Profi Birki 2.0 cites a European standard (EN ISO 20347), while US employers may ask for an ASTM test. Sanita separates its ASTM-certified styles from the rest of its Professional line. Match the exact standard your employer names.</p>
<p><strong>Can I wear a clog with vent holes on the line?</strong>
Vent holes are open gaps in the top of the shoe. If your kitchen has a footwear rule, ask whether it allows them.</p>
<p><strong>Should a kitchen clog feel loose?</strong>
Some should. Sanita, for example, says your heel should lift slightly as you walk. Other makers fit differently, so read each one&#39;s fit notes before you order.</p>
